# HRTO Damages Range: Failure to Accommodate Disability — Services Context

**Research question:** What is the range of monetary damages awarded for
discrimination based on failure to accommodate a disability in the context of
providing services (as opposed to employment)?

---

## 1. Analytical framework (not services-specific, but universally applied)

**Sanford v. Koop, 2005 HRTO 53** — an *employment* case (sexual harassment by
a supervisor), not a services case. Its value here is the analytical framework
it set out for quantifying injury to dignity, feelings and self-respect (paras
34–38), later applied across all Code social areas including services:

- Objective seriousness of the conduct
- Effect on the particular applicant
- Factors: humiliation, hurt feelings, loss of self-respect/dignity/confidence,
  victimization, vulnerability, seriousness/frequency/duration of treatment

This framework was refined in **Arunachalam v. Best Buy Canada, 2010 HRTO
1880** (two-criterion test: objective seriousness + personal impact) and
**ADGA Group Consultants Inc. v. Lane, 2008 CanLII 39605 (Div. Ct.)**
(no ceiling on damages; award must not be so low as to create a "licence fee"
to discriminate).

Both **Barton v. Halton** and **Daykin v. Carlisle Suites** (below) explicitly
apply the Sanford/Lane/Arunachalam framework to services-context facts —
confirming it is not employment-specific.

## 2. Services-context damages, by subtype

### (a) Denial of service-animal access (retail/restaurant — single incident)

Per **Daykin v. Carlisle Suites** (para. 31), the Tribunal has repeatedly
ordered **$500–$5,000** for denial of equal access to services/goods/facilities
because of reliance on a service animal:
- Smolak v. 1636764 Ontario, 2009 HRTO 1032
- Schussler v. 1709043 Ontario, 2009 HRTO 2194
- Hill v. Bani-Ahmad, 2014 HRTO 937
- Sweet v. 1790907 Ontario Inc., 2015 HRTO 433
- Sprague v. RioCan Empress Walk Inc., 2015 HRTO 942 (paras. 69–75 collect
  further comparators, range $200–$15,000, but all pre-2015 and none involve
  search-warrant-type facts)
- Gauthier v. Yonge Palace Guest House North York, 2019 HRTO 840

No ceiling: **Bourdeau v. Kingston Bazar, 2012 HRTO 393** — store owner
demanded a woman with a guide dog leave — **$15,000**.

*(Note: exact figures in each cited case not yet independently verified — the
$500–$5,000 range and the $15,000 outlier are as characterized by the Daykin
tribunal, not independently confirmed from each underlying decision.)*

### (b) Denial of housing/tenancy

- **Janes v. Wright, 2012 HRTO 1621** — denial of rental due to receipt of
  social assistance — **$7,500**
- **Thomas v. Haque, 2016 HRTO 1012** — denial of accommodation because of
  race, colour, place of origin — **$10,000**
- **Dwyer v. Tarantino, 2020 HRTO 685** — denial of housing because of race
  and association — **$7,500 each** to two applicants
- **Daykin v. Carlisle Suites, 2025 HRTO 3097** — denial of tenancy because of
  reliance on a Seizure Response Dog (disability: epilepsy) —
  **$25,000** injury to dignity + **$13,620** economic loss (rent
  differential + storage/moving expenses)

Daykin explicitly adjusts for inflation, noting the most recent housing
comparator (Dwyer, 2020) is dated, and that a 2016 award of $10,000 is not
equivalent to a 2025 award of $10,000 (citing Huard v. NCR Leasing, Madkour v.
Alabi, Clarke v. Mohamed Elsabakhawi Drugs).

### (c) Police services

- **Barton v. Halton (Police Services Board), 2026 HRTO 674** — during
  execution of a search warrant, applicant with PTSD was denied access to her
  service dog; police failed to consider the reasonable alternative of letting
  her wait in the backyard with the dog (procedural duty to accommodate
  breached) — **$40,000** injury to dignity to Mrs. Barton
  - Her husband's separate claim (disability was *a factor* in the search/
    seizure decision, not itself a failure-to-accommodate claim) —
    **$20,000**, benchmarked against racial-profiling comparators (Nassiah v.
    Peel Police, $20,000; Phipps v. TPS, $10,000; Maynard v. TPS, $40,000;
    Dungus v. TPS, $13,000; JKB v. Peel Police, $30,000) rather than
    disability/service-animal comparators, since the Tribunal treated it as
    closer to a racial-profiling fact pattern.

The Barton tribunal explicitly distinguished the $200–$15,000 retail/
restaurant service-animal range as outdated and not involving comparably
serious facts (execution of a search warrant vs. being asked to leave a
store) — a key reasoning step for arguing upward departure from the retail
range in more serious service contexts.

## 3. Working synthesis

| Context | Range (nominal $) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Service animal denied — retail/restaurant, single incident | $500–$15,000 | mostly pre-2019 decisions; $15,000 is the outlier high (Bourdeau) |
| Housing/tenancy denial (disability or other ground) | $7,500–$25,000 | Daykin (2025) is the most recent and highest; explicitly inflation-adjusted |
| Police services — service animal/disability-related | $20,000–$40,000 | Barton (2026); most recent and highest; Tribunal treated the search-warrant context as more serious than retail-context comparators |

**Trend:** more recent decisions (2025–2026) cluster meaningfully higher than
the pre-2020 service-animal comparators, and tribunals are explicitly
reasoning from (i) inflation adjustment and (ii) severity/context of the
service denied (a few minutes in a store vs. hours during a police search of
one's home) to justify departing upward from older ranges.

## 4. Gaps / next steps

- Underlying figures for Smolak, Schussler, Hill, Sweet, Sprague, Gauthier,
  Nassiah, Phipps, Maynard, Dungus, JKB have not been independently verified
  against the original decisions — only as characterized in Daykin/Barton.
  Worth pulling primary text if these become load-bearing citations.
- Consider searching specifically for **services-context** cases beyond
  service-animal fact patterns (e.g., failure to accommodate a communication
  disability, a mobility disability, in a services setting) to broaden beyond
  the service-animal cluster this search happened to surface.
- The original 233-case CanLII keyword scan mostly surfaced service-animal
  and housing fact patterns for the "granted" cases — worth a second targeted
  scan for other disability types in services contexts (e.g., "sign language
  interpretation", "wheelchair accessibility", "communication disability
  services") to check whether the range holds across disability types.
